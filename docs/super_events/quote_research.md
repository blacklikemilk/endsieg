# Super-event quote and remark research

Scope: the five super-events currently in design — the Compiègne armistice of 11 November 1918, the mirrored Central Powers victory, the fall of France in June 1940, the Russian collapse of 1917, and the end of the Spanish Civil War in 1939.

This sheet is research input for the quote (`q`) and response (`a`) slots. It does not reserve super-event IDs; register those through the procedure in `docs/super_events/README.md` before wiring anything.

## Method

Every line below was checked against a page that actually prints the wording, and the page was read rather than inferred from a search snippet. Where a speech exists in an official report, the report itself was read: the 1918 and 1940 Hansard pages were loaded in a browser and the text inspected directly, which is how two popular paraphrases were caught and corrected (see the Lloyd George and Churchill notes). Foreign-language quotes are given in the original, with the English rendering marked as ours, because the standard published translations are frequently still in copyright even when the original is not.

Confidence means confidence in the wording and the attribution, not in the quote's fitness for a panel.

- **high** — read verbatim in a primary document, official report, or archive-backed edition.
- **medium** — wording is stable across sources but the translation, date, or occasion varies between them.
- **low** — the wording is a later reconstruction. Do not print it with attribution.

Public-domain notes give the author's death year where the term depends on it. The working rule used here: 70 years post mortem for European authors, so the term expires at the end of the seventieth year and the work is free from 1 January of the following year. United States status is noted separately where it differs, since publication before 1929 settles most of the older material.

**Recommended quote (11 words):** "The cruellest and most terrible war that has ever scourged mankind."

This is a verbatim fragment of candidate 1, cut at a sentence boundary without altering a word. It carries the whole weight of the moment and reads as one line.

**Recommended response line (8 words):** "The guns fall silent at the eleventh hour."

Composed in period newspaper register, not a quotation. It echoes the phrasing every paper used that week without claiming to reproduce a specific headline.

**Rejected on verification.** Foch's "Ce n'est pas une paix, c'est un armistice de vingt ans" is the most tempting line for this event and it should not be used. Nothing earlier than Paul Reynaud's memoirs is on record; the *Oxford Dictionary of Quotations* assigns it to the 1960 French and 1963 English editions of those memoirs, and Reynaud was not present. Foch's aide-de-camp Charles Bugnet, writing in 1929, does not mention it, and Foch was at Kreuznach rather than Paris on 28 June 1919. A 1939 press anecdote has him saying "the war is postponed for twenty years" on 11 November 1918, which is a different sentence and also uncorroborated. Confidence in the familiar wording: low. See http://www.strangehistory.net/2016/07/11/foch-twenty-year-armistice-myth/

**Recommended quote (13 words):** "Securing the German Reich in the West and East for the foreseeable future."

Public-domain document, our translation. It is the 1914 war aim stated in the government's own words, so printing it at the moment that aim is achieved needs no editorial framing.

**Recommended response line (4 words):** "Peace in the west." German file: "Friede im Westen."

Composed in the register of a German government announcement, not a quotation.

**Note on candidates 2 and 3.** Both are true to the moment but neither is a victory line. Mann's sentence is about the war's outbreak, and Tacitus puts the words in the mouth of a defeated Briton, which reads as reproach rather than triumph. They work as alternative panels if the design wants irony rather than bureaucratic finality.

**Rejected on verification.** "Am deutschen Wesen soll die Welt genesen" is a real line from Emanuel Geibel's 1861 poem "Deutschlands Beruf" and is safely public domain, but its twentieth-century use as a nationalist slogan makes it unusable here regardless of the original context.

Chosen over the two more famous lines precisely because it is free. It is also the only one of the three spoken by the government that actually surrendered, which suits a super-event about the fall rather than the resistance to it.

**Recommended response line (3 words):** "L'armistice est signé."

Composed period newspaper line, not a quotation.

Use the Pétain line, or a public-domain substitute such as clause 3 of the Armistice of 22 June 1940, rather than either of the blocked lines.

## Method note for sections 6 to 9

The same rules apply as in section 1: every line was read on a page that prints it, foreign-language quotes are given in the original with the English marked as ours, and nothing is printed with an attribution the page does not support.

One new constraint governs the 1937 section. The quote box in `interface/endsieg_super_events.gui` (`super_event_quote_textbox`, `[GetendsiegSuperEventQuote]`) uses the font `hoi_18mbs`, and vanilla's `bitmapfont` definition for that name loads `gfx/fonts/hoi_18mbs` together with `gfx/fonts/hoi_18mbs_cryllic`, so Cyrillic renders in any language build and the Bulgarian line of section 8 can stand on the panel as it is. Chinese does not: `hoi_18mbs` carries CJK glyphs only through language-filtered overrides — `bitmapfont_override` for `l_japanese` in `interface/core.gfx`, and the `bitmapfont_override` set in `interface/core_chinese.gfx` — so a Chinese line in the quote slot renders as missing glyphs in an English, German, French, Spanish, Polish, Russian or Portuguese build. Print the English rendering on the panel for the 1937 event and keep the Chinese in this sheet and in the attribution. The description box uses `hoi4_typewriter22`, whose definition is Latin-only outside `l_russian`, `l_polish` and `l_japanese`; the same rule applies to any CJK text placed in the description.

**Recommended quote (13 words):** "Meine Herren, wir sind jetzt in der Notwehr; und Not kennt kein Gebot!"

This is the outbreak stated as a confession: the Chancellor of the Reich tells the Reichstag that a treaty is being broken and that necessity overrides law. It is verbatim, it is contemporaneous, and it needs no editorial framing on a dark panel. The German can stand on the panel as it is; put our English rendering in the English localisation.

**Recommended response line (4 words):** "Die Mobilmachung ist befohlen." English file: "Mobilisation has been ordered."

Composed in the register of a German government announcement, not a quotation. It is the sentence a newspaper prints on 1 August 1914, and it keeps the button flat against the confession above it.

**Considered and not tabled.** Grey's "The lamps are going out all over Europe, we shall not see them lit again in our life-time" is the most evocative line of the moment and it is public domain, but it is a memoir recollection rather than a contemporary record: Grey published it in *Twenty-Five Years 1892–1916* (1925), p. 20, and J. A. Spender identified himself as the friend in 1927, so the wording rests on two recollections of a remark made at a window in the dusk. Confidence: medium. It is also 26 words, which is over the panel budget. If the design wants it, use the shorter fragment "The lamps are going out all over Europe" and mark the attribution as a recollection. Background: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_lamps_are_going_out

**Note on candidate 2.** "Ich kenne keine Parteien mehr" is the Burgfrieden formula, and it reads as unity rather than as dread. It is the right line if the event is staged as the nation closing ranks; candidate 1 is the right line if it is staged as the moment the law was set aside.

**Recommended quote (10 characters, 12 words in the English rendering):** "地无分南北，年无分老幼"

This is the government's own sentence cut at a clause boundary, and it is the phrase the speech is remembered by. It states the war as a nation-wide levy rather than as a diplomatic incident, which is what the panel has to convey. Print "No distinction of north or south, no distinction of old or young." on the panel: `hoi_18mbs` has no CJK glyphs outside the Chinese and Japanese language builds, so the Chinese belongs in the attribution and in this sheet, not in the quote slot of an English build.

**Recommended response line (6 words):** "已到最后关头。" English file: "We have reached the last moment."

Composed, but built from the term the moment itself used: the Lushan speech circulated as 最后关头, "the last moment". It is a period headline rather than a quotation, and it should not be presented as one.

**Note on candidates 1 and 3.** The two documents disagree about what kind of event this was, and that disagreement is the research finding. Tokyo's cabinet decision of 11 July calls the fighting an incident (事件) caused by planned Chinese resistance and orders reinforcements while insisting on local settlement; Japan issued no declaration of war in July 1937. Chiang's statement of 17 July answers it with the language of a war of national survival. A super-event built on candidate 3 is a Japanese decision panel; one built on candidate 1 is a Chinese proclamation panel; the pairing is what makes the outbreak legible.

**Recommended response line (7 words):** "Нашето дело е право, велико и свято." English file: "Our cause is just, great and holy."

A sourced sentence from the same manifesto, used the way the Spanish section uses Franco's closing communiqué: as the state's own line rather than as the panel quotation. It is short enough for a button and it is exactly what a Sofia paper prints in October 1912.

**Rejected on verification.** "Der ganze Balkan ist nicht die Knochen eines einzigen pommerschen Grenadiers wert" is the version in circulation and it is wrong twice: Bismarck's sentence is conditional ("so lange ich … kein Interesse sehe") rather than a flat valuation of the peninsula, and the rank he named was Musketier, not Grenadier. The wording above is the one printed in the speech text.

**Note on candidate 3.** Bismarck spoke in 1876, thirty-six years before the war, so this is the Great Power attitude that made the war possible rather than a line about the war itself. It works as an ironic panel — the peninsula the Powers would not spend a musketeer on is now deciding their alliances — and it should not be presented as a 1912 utterance.

**Note on dates.** Montenegro opened hostilities on 8 October 1912; Bulgaria, Serbia and Greece followed on 18 October. The manifesto is dated in the Julian calendar, which is why Bulgarian sources give 5 October and Western ones 18 October for the same document.

**Recommended quote (11 words):** "War between Christians would be shameful after the war of liberation."

This is the last argument in the room before Bulgaria attacked, and it is the whole tragedy of 1913 in one sentence: the alliance that had just beaten the Ottomans turned on itself within a month of the peace. Attribution on the panel should read for what it is — Theodorov, in the Bulgarian council, as recorded by the Carnegie Commission — because the wording is the Commission's rendering and not a stenographic record.

**Recommended response line (7 words):** "The allies have turned on one another."

Composed in newspaper register, not a quotation. It is also the plainest true statement available about 29 June 1913.

**Note on the missing document.** There is no Bulgarian declaration of war to quote. Bulgaria attacked the Serbian and Greek lines on the night of 29–30 June 1913 without a declaration, which is why the closest thing to an official voice for the outbreak is the cabinet discussion the Commission reconstructed, and why the Commission's Introduction calls the result fratricidal.

**Note on candidate 3.** Trotsky was in the Balkans as a correspondent through both wars, and his sentence is the only one of the three written by a witness rather than by a commission or a minister. It is also about the settlement rather than the outbreak, so it works better as a description line under the panel than as the panel itself.

## Open points

Two of the three French candidates and one Spanish line are blocked or weakened by attribution problems, so the French event has the thinnest safety margin of the five and a fourth verified public-domain candidate would be worth adding before that panel is locked.

The Central Powers victory is the only event with no contemporary quotation about the outcome itself, because the outcome never happened. Its candidates are therefore documents and literature about the war's purpose, and the panel will read as intent rather than as report.

Button lines are composed in period voice except where marked as a sourced slogan or a sourced official sentence; none of the composed lines should be presented anywhere as a historical quotation.
